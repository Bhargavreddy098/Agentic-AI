# Product lens — local-first agent workspace

**Score: 4/10. Vote: FUND IF.** Fund a tightly bounded demand experiment; do not fund the platform roadmap on the supplied evidence. This is an independent application of the Product framework summarized from Walter Isaacson's *Steve Jobs* (2011) and Apple's public product history, not an impersonation or endorsement.

## What is strong

The most promising product moment is concrete: a user delegates a real task, leaves it running, returns to an understandable result, and can recover a failure without reconstructing context. Observation, stop, inspection, and resume could make autonomy feel usable. That is a stronger experience hypothesis than “all your models in one interface.” Whether it improves real completion or user effort is **unknown**.

Local files, durable history, and provider choice could support ownership and continuity. These are supplied design intentions, not verified user benefits. Local-first matters only if a selected customer actually needs local handling or continuity enough to accept installation, machine availability, setup, and support costs. Neither that need nor willingness to pay is established. Local storage alone does not establish privacy when task content goes to a cloud model or tool.

Premium craft could make difficult agent work legible: one clear task state, understandable permissions, useful progress, a trustworthy output, and an explicit explanation when recovery is impossible. Hidden quality matters equally: durable run state, safe handling of secrets, reliable tool execution, and honest accounting of what happened. Those are necessary product work, not optional polish after the interface ships.

The chair's primary-source check establishes advertised overlap: [Codex](https://openai.com/index/introducing-the-codex-app/) and [Claude Code desktop](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/desktop) already describe graphical parallel agent/session work, [Hermes](https://github.com/NousResearch/hermes-agent) describes provider choice, memory, tools, and subagents, and [OpenClaw](https://docs.openclaw.ai/web/control-ui) describes a browser control UI with live tool progress. This does not establish equivalent quality or a fully solved customer workflow. It does mean the product must demonstrate a better specific experience; the mere existence of these components is insufficient differentiation.

**Strongest version, in one sentence:** “Delegate a recurring research task over your local project files, then inspect, correct, and continue the work in one place.” This is a proposed starting wedge, not a proven customer choice. Narrow it further to one buyer and one output before implementation. The provider/runtime machinery earns its place only when it serves that outcome.

## What would kill it — top three ranked

1. **The customer does not urgently need to change their working habit.** The brief names many tool users but supplies no recent task where fragmentation caused a costly, repeated failure. A user may tolerate several tools because each already fits a familiar job; consolidation can impose its own migration and learning cost. Actual switching, pain frequency, retention, and payment are all **unknown**. Excellent engineering can still produce a destination people admire in demos and never reopen. The proposed first assumption to validate is: **a narrowly identified user has a recurring task whose current handoffs hurt enough that they will move that task into this workspace and return voluntarily.**

2. **The product exposes its architecture as the user's job.** Providers, per-agent models, agents, tools, memory, workflows, subagents, and monitoring describe a system inventory. If setup requires users to understand that inventory before receiving value, the product has failed the first-five-minute test. “Genuine agents” is a technical constraint; it is not yet a customer promise. A small team attempting every subsystem risks many adequate settings screens and no exceptional completed task. Cut the general workflow builder, configurable agent hierarchy, memory management UI, broad provider matrix, and optional cloud/sync/remote execution from the first demand test. Preserve a minimal control only when the chosen task requires it. Provider independence can remain an architectural constraint without becoming an onboarding ceremony.

3. **The seams undermine trust and erase the apparent simplicity.** Model credentials, local model installation, cloud quotas, tool permissions, filesystem paths, process restarts, and partial side effects all interrupt the experience. A polished shell cannot compensate for an opaque stall or a retry that repeats an external action. Supporting both local and cloud execution also creates different performance and capability expectations; their interchangeability is **unknown**. The founder cannot control every model, but must own diagnosis, expectation setting, and recovery. Reliability evidence, supported recovery semantics, support burden, and the team's ability to deliver these are **unknown**. If the team spends all its capacity accommodating provider differences, the promised whole experience never materializes.

## Questions for founder

- Who is the first buyer, what specific recurring task did they last perform, and where did the current tool handoff fail? Obtain actual examples and artifacts rather than asking whether an integrated workspace sounds useful.
- What single finished output should appear in the first five minutes, using the buyer's material? What may require longer, and how will progress remain understandable? Time to value is currently **unknown**.
- Which capabilities will you explicitly refuse to build until that buyer returns? If the answer remains “many kinds of people doing autonomous tasks,” the product has no focus.
- What does the buyer lose by switching: existing context, scripts, trusted habits, extensions, speed, or predictability? These are investigation prompts, not asserted competitor limitations. No verified feature gap in the named alternatives is established by this memo.
- What are you promising when the user clicks resume after a crash or partial tool execution? Which actions can safely repeat, which need inspection, and which cannot resume? Demonstrate the promise on the chosen workflow.
- What is the paid value after users bring and fund their own models? The candidate is repeated successful delegation with less setup, supervision, and recovery effort. Whether users value and purchase that outcome is **unknown**; model access, visual polish, and provider choice alone do not answer it.
- What improves through sustained use that is difficult to reproduce? A distinctive trusted workflow, accumulated task context under the user's control, and consistently excellent recovery might create attachment. These are hypotheses. A unified interface and local-first positioning alone establish no defensibility.

## FUND IF conditions

**Validate the switching assumption first.** Recruit people from one named role who already perform the same recurring task. Observe their current workflow, including time spent configuring, handing work between tools, verifying, and recovering. Ask them to supply their next real instance. Unknowns to resolve are recurrence, material cost of friction, and whether a new workspace reduces total effort rather than merely relocating it.

Build a constrained end-to-end prototype for that task. The first encounter should communicate one outcome, require only the credentials and permissions necessary to deliver it, run on real user material, expose useful progress, and produce an inspectable artifact. Exercise a realistic interruption and recovery. Use one supported execution path initially; broaden provider support only when observed demand warrants it. Do not imply that partially mocked or founder-operated steps are already autonomous.

Compare the prototype against the buyer's actual current combination of tools. Measure completion quality, user supervision, setup effort, failure recovery, and voluntary reuse. A demonstration on a curated task proves feasibility only. A simulated consumer panel cannot prove switching or willingness to pay.

**Proposed experiment decision rules, not observed rates or market estimates:** recruit five qualified users with a near-term repeat task; require at least three to finish two separate real task instances, with the second initiated voluntarily and without live founder intervention. Record failures and total founder assistance. Treat this as permission for another bounded experiment, not evidence of broad product-market fit. If the task repeats slowly, extend the observation window rather than manufacture retention.

Before a substantial platform build, present a concrete, honestly scoped paid pilot offer and seek actual commitments. Set its commercial terms from the observed job and cost to deliver; this memo invents no price. Explicitly distinguish praise, waitlist signups, acceptance of free assistance, and completed purchases. Payment evidence, delivery economics, and repeat use must all inform the next decision.

The product earns further funding when one narrowly defined customer repeatedly chooses its completed experience over their existing workflow, and the small team can deliver that experience reliably. Until then, the platform breadth is a liability disguised as ambition.
